2.1 Current IAQ Management Approaches and Limitations

The existing approaches to managing indoor air quality include source control, outdoor-air ventilation and air cleaning. Their effectiveness depends on the pollutants being addressed and the conditions within the space. Table 2 summarises the approaches relevant to the four air quality parameters considered in this project.

Table 2: Current IAQ management approaches and its limitations (EPA, 2018; NEA, 2026)
ParameterCurrent approachesMain limitations
CO₂Outdoor-air ventilation through heating, ventilation and air-conditioning (HVAC) systemsIn hot and humid conditions, additional outdoor air increases the cooling and dehumidification load.
PM2.5Air purifiers containing high efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filtersPerformance depends on airflow, equipment sizing, placement and filter maintenance.
TVOCsSource control, HVAC systems and gas-phase filtration using activated carbon or other suitable adsorbentsRemoval varies between compounds and filter media. Adsorbents have finite capacity and require replacement.
FormaldehydeLower-emission materials, outdoor-air ventilation and media specifically suited to formaldehyde removalExisting materials may continue emitting formaldehyde. Removal performance depends on the treatment method, media capacity and operating conditions.

As illustrated in Figure 3, HVAC systems with outdoor-air ventilation help dilute CO₂ and TVOCs generated indoors. Part of the return air is exhausted, while the remainder mixes with incoming outdoor air before being filtered, cooled and supplied back to the room. When pollutant generation increases, greater outdoor-air exchange may be needed to maintain indoor concentrations. In Singapore’s hot and humid climate, the additional outdoor air requires cooling and dehumidification, which can increase energy demand (Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, n.d.).

Air cleaning can supplement ventilation through HEPA filters for PM2.5 and suitable adsorbents, such as activated carbon, for selected gaseous pollutants. Their effectiveness depends on airflow, the pollutants present and filter maintenance (EPA, 2018). These approaches therefore require consideration of treatment performance, energy demand, maintenance and available space.

2.2 Active Botanical Filtration and Supporting Research

The approaches discussed in Section 2.1 highlight the need to balance pollutant removal with energy demand and maintenance requirements. These considerations motivate the investigation of botanical filtration as a supplementary treatment method. By combining plants, their growing medium and associated microorganisms, botanical systems offer the potential to address multiple pollutants within a single system (Matheson et al., 2023).

Figure 4 illustrates the complementary processes involved in phytoremediation, which uses plants and their associated microorganisms to remove or break down pollutants. Leaves can take up certain gaseous pollutants, while the growing medium can retain airborne particles and adsorb some gases. Microorganisms associated with plant roots can biodegrade certain organic pollutants. Plants also absorb CO₂ through photosynthesis. The contribution of each process therefore varies with the pollutant and growing conditions (Matheson et al., 2023).

Active botanical systems incorporate fans to increase contact between polluted air and the treatment medium. Figure 5 shows a configuration in which a fan draws air into the base and directs it upwards through the central structure. Air then passes through the growing substrate within the surrounding planter modules before returning to the room (Smith et al., 2023). Unlike passive potted plants, this arrangement provides forced airflow through the planted medium, bringing pollutants into contact with the substrate and root-associated microorganisms.

Experimental research supports the potential of this approach. Pongkua et al. (2024) investigated an active living wall planted with Sansevieria trifasciata in a 24 m³ chamber using cigarette smoke as the pollutant source (Figure 6). The authors reported more than 90% particulate matter removal within 12 hours, while removal of individual VOCs ranged from approximately 25–80%, including approximately 80% for formaldehyde. These findings demonstrate the potential to combine particulate and gaseous pollutant treatment, although the reported efficiencies reflect the study’s experimental conditions and require further validation for the intended workspaces.

Beyond pollutant removal, botanical systems can provide a biophilic element by bringing living plants into indoor environments. Nieuwenhuis et al. (2014) found that enriching offices with plants improved workplace satisfaction and self-reported concentration. Combining these potential benefits with multiple pollutant-treatment mechanisms makes botanical filtration relevant to the selected workspaces. It is therefore investigated as an integrated supplementary approach.

2.3 Existing Botanical Filtration Systems

The botanical filtration principles discussed in Section 2.2 have been incorporated into commercial systems. Naava and Nedlaw illustrate two approaches to integrating plants and engineered airflow into indoor environments, with different configurations and installation requirements.

Naava, a Finnish company, offers freestanding vertical green walls that combine botanical air treatment with indoor greenery. These units can also serve as room dividers, allowing them to be incorporated into spaces such as offices, schools and public areas (Figure 7). Fans circulate air through the planted growing medium, while irrigation and lighting support plant growth. This furniture-like configuration integrates botanical filtration into the layout of an occupied room (Naava, n.d.).

Nedlaw’s custom-designed living-wall biofilters illustrate a larger-scale approach, with installations in atriums and institutional buildings (Figure 8). Air is drawn through the planted wall using onboard fans or a connection to the building’s HVAC system. Each installation is designed around requirements such as available space, airflow and lighting, enabling the biofilter to form part of the building’s air-treatment infrastructure (Nedlaw Living Walls, n.d.).

These examples demonstrate how botanical filtration can be incorporated into both freestanding units and architectural installations. Their differing configurations provide a basis for considering the space requirements, installation arrangements and maintenance needs relevant to the small enclosed workspaces targeted in this project.

2.4 Challenges in Existing Botanical Systems

The systems discussed in Section 2.3 demonstrate the practical application of botanical filtration. However, their suitability for small enclosed workspaces requires further consideration. Four challenges are particularly relevant to this project: deployment flexibility, performance observability, response to changing pollutant levels and room-scale performance.

Limited deployment flexibility: Large, custom-designed living walls require installation arrangements suited to the building, including sufficient space, lighting and airflow provision (Nedlaw Living Walls, n.d.). Freestanding systems offer flexibility in placement, but their dimensions and treatment capacity must still suit the room. For the selected 100 - 300 ft² workspaces, the challenge is to accommodate different layouts and treatment requirements while preserving usable floor area.

Limited performance observability: Users need accessible information to understand how indoor pollutant levels change during operation. However, visual appeal can receive greater attention than air-cleaning performance. Shen et al. (2024) noted that interior green-wall design often prioritises aesthetics and cost, with insufficient consideration of plant species’ VOC-removal capabilities. This highlights the need to support botanical systems’ visual appeal with clearly presented measurements that help users assess their contribution to indoor air quality.

Unclear response to changing pollutant levels: Indoor emissions vary with activities and occupancy, as illustrated by the nail salon scenario in Section 1.3. A relevant question is how botanical systems adjust their operation when these conditions change. Although existing products mention incorporating automated functions, the relationship between measurements of the selected pollutants and adjustments to treatment airflow requires examination. The challenge is to determine an appropriate operating response to changing treatment demand.

Uncertain room-scale performance: Experimental removal efficiencies depend on factors such as pollutant concentration, airflow and test duration. Matheson et al. (2023) highlight the need for further assessment in actual indoor environments containing mixed pollutant sources. Performance in the selected workspaces therefore needs to be established under representative conditions, accounting for room volume, ventilation and recurring emissions. Such evaluation is necessary to determine whether the system provides a measurable contribution to indoor air treatment.
