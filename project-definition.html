3.1 Problem Statement and Objective

Small enclosed workplaces with limited natural ventilation can experience recurring exposure to multiple pollutants released by activities, materials and occupants, raising concerns about health, comfort and work performance. Many individual treatment technologies are pollutant-specific, such as HEPA filtration for particulate matter and adsorbents for specific gases. Active botanical filtration was therefore selected for investigation because it offers the potential to address particles and certain gaseous pollutants within one integrated plant-substrate system.

However, its suitability for the intended spaces remains uncertain due to:

Limited deployment flexibility: Fixed dimensions or positions can restrict deployment across room layouts and relocation nearer to pollutant sources.

Limited visibility of IAQ performance: Insufficient accessible information makes changing pollutant levels and treatment effects difficult to assess.

Unclear responsiveness: The relationship between sensor readings and system operation is not sufficiently established for the intended application.

Uncertain treatment capacity: Performance under the intended room sizes, ventilation conditions and pollutant sources remains insufficiently verified.

The project therefore aims to help operators of enclosed workplaces with limited natural ventilation manage recurring indoor pollutants through adaptable botanical air treatment that displays changes in pollutant levels, responds to changing conditions and provides experimentally verified treatment capacity within a defined operating range.

3.2 Project Scope

The project covers the design, development and evaluation of an adaptable botanical air-treatment system for small enclosed workplaces with limited natural ventilation. The intended application range is 100–300 ft² (approximately 9.29–27.87 m²), with suitability to be assessed through testing under defined room and operating conditions.

Development includes the physical structure, airflow delivery and conditions needed to sustain plant operation, including lighting and irrigation. The work also covers monitoring for automation and displaying indoor air conditions. Building on an existing botanical treatment approach, the team’s contribution focuses on engineering development, integration and experimental evaluation. Testing will investigate pollutant removal under different operating conditions and room sizes to establish treatment capacity and inform sizing guidance. The intended deliverables are a functional prototype, measured performance data and recommendations for use within the tested range.

Treatment evaluation will address PM2.5 and selected gaseous pollutants (TVOCs), including formaldehyde. CO₂, temperature and relative humidity will provide additional operating context. The system is intended to supplement ventilation and source control. Microbial strain development, comprehensive plant-species screening and demonstration of health outcomes are outside the project scope.

Future work will be divided by subsystem, with each lead responsible for development, testing and subsequent improvements in their area. This supports parallel progress through the prototype development and design refinement phases shown in the Gantt chart (see Figure 25). System integration and whole-system testing will be undertaken collaboratively.

3.4 Measurable Requirements

Table 5 consolidates the current design targets and their basis. Literature and commercial products provide benchmarks, but performance reported for another plant-substrate system is not evidence that the proposed system already achieves the same result. Numerical targets remain provisional until their operating and measurement conditions are specified and tested. Single Pass Removal Efficiency (SPRE) is used as target purification performance, refer to Appendix 2 for detailed definition of the term.

Table 5: Measurable Requirements

Requirement

Working target

Basis

Assembly footprint

≤ 5% of room floor area

Space-allocation target, informed by current existing living wall (NAAVA one) dimensions. The model takes up approximately 3.78% of a 100 ft² space (see Appendix 1). A higher target was set to accommodate plants.

Assembly height

2.4m–2.6m

Minimum height is determined by BCA (2025, p. 15). Maximum height is a planning assumption based on 100–300 ft² indoor spaces in Singapore, not a universal permissible assembly height.

Module dimensions

L + W + H ≤ 150 cm (Linear Dimension)

Based on Technical Reference (TR) 105:2022 (Guidelines for last mile delivery of parcels), which covers domestic parcels with combined length, breadth and height not exceeding 150 cm. This informs the module’s packed size for individual delivery (Enterprise Singapore, 2022, pp. 4–5).

According to Scoot's checked-baggage linear-dimension limit (≤ 158 cm) as a benchmark (FlyScoot, n.d.).

Module Weight

≤ 25 kg

Singapore Standards (SS) 569:2011 (Code of practice for manual handling) recommends a maximum of 25 kg for short-duration, low-frequency lifting under ideal conditions, including keeping the load close to the body, good two-handed grip and no twisting. This informs an initial upper reference (Enterprise Singapore, 2011, p. 35).

Additional support is drawn from TR 105:2022 (Guidelines for last mile delivery of parcels), which covers parcels weighing no more than 25 kg or one-third of the handler’s body weight, whichever is lighter (Enterprise Singapore, 2022, pp. 4–5).

PM2.5 removal

SPRE ≥ 55%

Irga et al. (2023) reported PM2.5 SPRE of 56.42 ± 21.02% for a moveable active green wall. This is used as a comparison benchmark, recognising the reported variability and different systems.

TVOCs removal

SPRE ≥ 55%

Torpy et al. (2017) reported approximately 57% SPRE for an active living wall tested with methyl ethyl ketone (MEK), a VOC. This provides a comparison benchmark.

Formaldehyde removal

SPRE ≥ 30%

Wu et al. (2026) reported formaldehyde SPRE of 24.75–34.5% for an active green-wall module under different airflow conditions. A 30% target is selected within this reported range as a comparison benchmark.

Outlet air speed (Plant-facing)

≤ 1 m/s

Franco et al. (2012) limited active living-wall inlet velocities to 1.0 m/s, noting potential mechanical damage to vegetation at higher speeds.

Sabeh (2024) also recommends 0.35–1.0 m/s for air movement across plants

Water-refill interval

≥ 30 days

Mobilane’s LivePanel TANK is an irrigation system for indoor living walls, using an integrated reservoir and pump to provide watering for an advertised 4–6 weeks per fill (Mobilane, n.d.). This supports a practical refill-interval benchmark.

Operating noise

≤ 50 dB(A)

Retained from SS 553:2026 (Code of Practice for Air-Conditioning and Mechanical Ventilation in buildings) ambient noise recommendation. Choosing office and retail shop standards as a benchmark (Enterprise Singapore, 2026).
