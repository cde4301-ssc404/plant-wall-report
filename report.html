Abstract

Small enclosed workplaces with limited natural ventilation can experience recurring exposure to particulate and gaseous pollutants, while restricted floor space complicates the installation of air-treatment systems. This project investigates a modular active botanical air-treatment system for workspaces of approximately 100–300 ft² (9.3–27.9 m²), intended to supplement ventilation and source control. Using Flora Aer (Botanical Filtration prototype) as a reference and retaining philodendron scandens, the proposed design integrates repeatable plant modules, supporting base modules and a separate air-quality sensor module. Fan-assisted airflow through the planted growing medium, distributed lighting and automated irrigation support treatment operation, while sensor feedback is intended to enable pollutant monitoring and threshold-based operation control.

Work completed includes concept selection, detailed module design, component shortlisting, preliminary mechanical prototyping and qualitative computational fluid dynamics comparisons. Three airflow configurations were investigated, with the second perforated-baffle design showing a more uniform simulated airflow distribution across the pot openings and being selected for prototype evaluation. These findings remain preliminary; pollutant-removal performance and compliance with design requirements have not yet been experimentally verified.

The next phase will assemble and verify the functional prototype before conducting experiments on fan operating settings, room configuration and module count. Measurements of pollutant-specific removal efficiency, clean air delivery rate and effective room treatment rate will inform design refinement and a sizing guide linking module configurations to treatment capacity within the tested operating range.

1. Introduction

1.1 Significance of Indoor Air Quality

Indoor Air Quality (IAQ) describes the condition of air within buildings in relation to the people who occupy them. IAQ is assessed by measuring airborne pollutant concentrations and environmental parameters such as temperature, relative humidity and comparing the measurements with the relevant recommended limits (NEA, n.d.). Good IAQ is crucial to human health and well-being, with implications for respiratory and cardiovascular health as well as cognitive performance (EPA, 2026; Allen et al., 2016). However, indoor air pollution remains a major yet overlooked concern, while outdoor sources such as urban traffic and industrial emissions receive greater attention. A 2025 World Health Organization (WHO) publication cited an estimated 3.2 million deaths annually associated with household air pollution from polluting cooking fuels (Cowell & Singh, 2025). More broadly, daily activities such as cooking, heating and cleaning can release pollutants that accumulate when ventilation is inadequate, often without visible signs (EPA, 2026).

In Singapore, the hot and humid climate encourages reliance on air-conditioning in indoor environments such as shopping malls, homes, schools and offices. People in Singapore have been estimated to spend approximately 90% of their time indoors (Qing, 2023). In these spaces, insufficient outdoor air-ventilation can allow pollutants from daily activities, furnishings and equipment to accumulate despite the room being cooled. Dyson’s connected-purifier study found that Singapore’s average indoor PM2.5 concentration in 2022 was 36% higher than outdoor levels, ranking fourth among the countries studied for this disparity (Dyson, 2024). These findings reinforce the importance of maintaining good IAQ. Local concerns were further highlighted in a 2024 report describing a family whose children repeatedly fell ill in a home with elevated formaldehyde levels associated with wooden furnishings (Seah & Tang, 2024). This underscores the relevance of the National Environment Agency’s guidance on maintaining adequate ventilation and adapting IAQ management measures to each space’s occupancy, activities and environmental conditions (NEA, 2026).

1.3 Coexistence and Variation of Indoor Pollutants

Indoor air pollutants can coexist within the same space because several sources may be present simultaneously. Nail salons would be one such example. Occupants release CO₂ through respiration, nail-care products emit VOCs, and nail filing generates airborne dust. Certain nail-care products may also emit formaldehyde, depending on their composition (OSHA, n.d.). These sources contribute to a mixture of gaseous and particulate pollutants within the same indoor environment.

2.2 Active Botanical Filtration and Supporting Research

The approaches discussed in Section 2.1 highlight the need to balance pollutant removal with energy demand and maintenance requirements. These considerations motivate the investigation of botanical filtration as a supplementary treatment method. By combining plants, their growing medium and associated microorganisms, botanical systems offer the potential to address multiple pollutants within a single system (Matheson et al., 2023).

Figure 4 illustrates the complementary processes involved in phytoremediation, which uses plants and their associated microorganisms to remove or break down pollutants. Leaves can take up certain gaseous pollutants, while the growing medium can retain airborne particles and adsorb some gases. Microorganisms associated with plant roots can biodegrade certain organic pollutants. Plants also absorb CO₂ through photosynthesis. The contribution of each process therefore varies with the pollutant and growing conditions (Matheson et al., 2023).

Note: Illustration generated using ChatGPT (OpenAI, 2026)

Active botanical systems incorporate fans to increase contact between polluted air and the treatment medium. Figure 5 shows a configuration in which a fan draws air into the base and directs it upwards through the central structure. Air then passes through the growing substrate within the surrounding planter modules before returning to the room (Smith et al., 2023). Unlike passive potted plants, this arrangement provides forced airflow through the planted medium, bringing pollutants into contact with the substrate and root-associated microorganisms.

Experimental research supports the potential of this approach. Pongkua et al. (2024) investigated an active living wall planted with Sansevieria trifasciata in a 24 m³ chamber using cigarette smoke as the pollutant source (Figure 6). The authors reported more than 90% particulate matter removal within 12 hours, while removal of individual VOCs ranged from approximately 25–80%, including approximately 80% for formaldehyde. These findings demonstrate the potential to combine particulate and gaseous pollutant treatment, although the reported efficiencies reflect the study’s experimental conditions and require further validation for the intended workspaces.

Beyond pollutant removal, botanical systems can provide a biophilic element by bringing living plants into indoor environments. Nieuwenhuis et al. (2014) found that enriching offices with plants improved workplace satisfaction and self-reported concentration. Combining these potential benefits with multiple pollutant-treatment mechanisms makes botanical filtration relevant to the selected workspaces. It is therefore investigated as an integrated supplementary approach.

2.3 Existing Botanical Filtration Systems

The botanical filtration principles discussed in Section 2.2 have been incorporated into commercial systems. Naava and Nedlaw illustrate two approaches to integrating plants and engineered airflow into indoor environments, with different configurations and installation requirements.

Naava, a Finnish company, offers freestanding vertical green walls that combine botanical air treatment with indoor greenery. These units can also serve as room dividers, allowing them to be incorporated into spaces such as offices, schools and public areas (Figure 7). Fans circulate air through the planted growing medium, while irrigation and lighting support plant growth. This furniture-like configuration integrates botanical filtration into the layout of an occupied room (Naava, n.d.).

Nedlaw’s custom-designed living-wall biofilters illustrate a larger-scale approach, with installations in atriums and institutional buildings (Figure 8). Air is drawn through the planted wall using onboard fans or a connection to the building’s HVAC system. Each installation is designed around requirements such as available space, airflow and lighting, enabling the biofilter to form part of the building’s air-treatment infrastructure (Nedlaw Living Walls, n.d.).

These examples demonstrate how botanical filtration can be incorporated into both freestanding units and architectural installations. Their differing configurations provide a basis for considering the space requirements, installation arrangements and maintenance needs relevant to the small enclosed workspaces targeted in this project.

2.4 Challenges in Existing Botanical Systems

The systems discussed in Section 2.3 demonstrate the practical application of botanical filtration. However, their suitability for small enclosed workspaces requires further consideration. Four challenges are particularly relevant to this project: deployment flexibility, performance observability, response to changing pollutant levels and room-scale performance.

Limited deployment flexibility: Large, custom-designed living walls require installation arrangements suited to the building, including sufficient space, lighting and airflow provision (Nedlaw Living Walls, n.d.). Freestanding systems offer flexibility in placement, but their dimensions and treatment capacity must still suit the room. For the selected 100 - 300 ft² workspaces, the challenge is to accommodate different layouts and treatment requirements while preserving usable floor area.

Limited performance observability: Users need accessible information to understand how indoor pollutant levels change during operation. However, visual appeal can receive greater attention than air-cleaning performance. Shen et al. (2024) noted that interior green-wall design often prioritises aesthetics and cost, with insufficient consideration of plant species’ VOC-removal capabilities. This highlights the need to support botanical systems’ visual appeal with clearly presented measurements that help users assess their contribution to indoor air quality.

Unclear response to changing pollutant levels: Indoor emissions vary with activities and occupancy, as illustrated by the nail salon scenario in Section 1.3. A relevant question is how botanical systems adjust their operation when these conditions change. Although existing products mention incorporating automated functions, the relationship between measurements of the selected pollutants and adjustments to treatment airflow requires examination. The challenge is to determine an appropriate operating response to changing treatment demand.

Uncertain room-scale performance: Experimental removal efficiencies depend on factors such as pollutant concentration, airflow and test duration. Matheson et al. (2023) highlight the need for further assessment in actual indoor environments containing mixed pollutant sources. Performance in the selected workspaces therefore needs to be established under representative conditions, accounting for room volume, ventilation and recurring emissions. Such evaluation is necessary to determine whether the system provides a measurable contribution to indoor air treatment.

3. Project Definition

3.1 Problem Statement and Objective

Small enclosed workplaces with limited natural ventilation can experience recurring exposure to multiple pollutants released by activities, materials and occupants, raising concerns about health, comfort and work performance. Many individual treatment technologies are pollutant-specific, such as HEPA filtration for particulate matter and adsorbents for specific gases. Active botanical filtration was therefore selected for investigation because it offers the potential to address particles and certain gaseous pollutants within one integrated plant-substrate system.

However, its suitability for the intended spaces remains uncertain due to:

Limited deployment flexibility: Fixed dimensions or positions can restrict deployment across room layouts and relocation nearer to pollutant sources.

Limited visibility of IAQ performance: Insufficient accessible information makes changing pollutant levels and treatment effects difficult to assess.

Unclear responsiveness: The relationship between sensor readings and system operation is not sufficiently established for the intended application.

Uncertain treatment capacity: Performance under the intended room sizes, ventilation conditions and pollutant sources remains insufficiently verified.

The project therefore aims to help operators of enclosed workplaces with limited natural ventilation manage recurring indoor pollutants through adaptable botanical air treatment that displays changes in pollutant levels, responds to changing conditions and provides experimentally verified treatment capacity within a defined operating range.

3.2 Project Scope

The project covers the design, development and evaluation of an adaptable botanical air-treatment system for small enclosed workplaces with limited natural ventilation. The intended application range is 100–300 ft² (approximately 9.29–27.87 m²), with suitability to be assessed through testing under defined room and operating conditions.

Development includes the physical structure, airflow delivery and conditions needed to sustain plant operation, including lighting and irrigation. The work also covers monitoring for automation and displaying indoor air conditions. Building on an existing botanical treatment approach, the team’s contribution focuses on engineering development, integration and experimental evaluation. Testing will investigate pollutant removal under different operating conditions and room sizes to establish treatment capacity and inform sizing guidance. The intended deliverables are a functional prototype, measured performance data and recommendations for use within the tested range.

Treatment evaluation will address PM2.5 and selected gaseous pollutants (TVOCs), including formaldehyde. CO₂, temperature and relative humidity will provide additional operating context. The system is intended to supplement ventilation and source control. Microbial strain development, comprehensive plant-species screening and demonstration of health outcomes are outside the project scope.

Future work will be divided by subsystem, with each lead responsible for development, testing and subsequent improvements in their area. This supports parallel progress through the prototype development and design refinement phases shown in the Gantt chart (see Figure 25). System integration and whole-system testing will be undertaken collaboratively.

4. Concept Development

4.1 Reference System and Plant

The existing Flora Aer prototype serves as the reference system for this project. It combines fan-assisted airflow through a planted growing medium with supplementary lighting and mist irrigation. Figure 9 shows its planting face and rear arrangement, providing a practical reference for accommodating the plants and integrating the services needed to sustain their operation.

The prototype uses Philodendron scandens, with supporting research also reported under Philodendron hederaceum. Suárez-Cáceres & Patricia (2021) tested a planted felt wall in a sealed chamber under formaldehyde exposure, recording about 83% reduction in TVOC readings after 15 minutes and 96% after one hour, relative to the empty-chamber baseline. These measurements used a TVOC detector rather than a formaldehyde-specific instrument. For PM₂.₅, Patil & Patil (2026) investigated a passive green wall planted with Philodendron scandens in an occupied office. Average PM2.5 concentrations decreased from approximately 34 to 28 µg/m³, with a reported reduction of 15.7%. This provides supporting evidence from passive planting (non-active) rather than direct validation of fan-assisted filtration.

Adopting the Flora Aer plant system provides a consistent basis for engineering development. Reference measurements inform pot accommodation and planting spacing (see Appendix 3), while plant requirements guide lighting and irrigation.

5. Detailed engineering design and development

5.1 Plant-module construction

Each module has a 600 mm × 600 mm planting face and a 250 mm-deep enclosure, as shown in Figure 13. Thirteen 80 mm-diameter pots are arranged in alternating rows of three and two. A rear fan supplies air through an internal chamber and perforated baffle before it passes through the planted pots. Module-level lighting is supported in front of the planting face; its projection is additional to the enclosure depth.

Construction combines laser-cut and 3D-printed components. Prototype assembly will verify pot fit, sealing, maintenance access and load transfer through the supporting structure under wet operating conditions. Development of the inter-module connection, including linkage and rack-and-pinion motion transfer and alternative interlocking interfaces, is documented in Appendix 4. The estimated module mass (derived from SolidWorks) is 17.5 kg excluding plants. Component-level mass accounting and wet-module weighing will establish whether the complete module meets the provisional 25 kg handling target.

5.2 Irrigation design

The irrigation system supplies water from the base reservoir through a shared pump and inter-module pipe connectors and pipework to misting nozzles within each plant module as seen in Figure 14. The staggered pot arrangement provides access for mist distribution, with watering uniformity to be verified experimentally.

Pump selection will account for delivery pressure, nozzle demand and the number and elevation of connected modules. Usable reservoir capacity will be assessed against measured consumption and losses to evaluate the 30-day refill target. Drainage, flow management, leakage detection and reservoir monitoring remain under development.

Calibration will establish nozzle flow, misting duration and cycle intervals. Airflow tests will use defined substrate moisture conditions, with irrigation timing, temperature and relative humidity recorded. Outlet velocities will be checked across the expected operating conditions against the provisional plant-airflow limit.

Net water consumption will be measured under the intended irrigation schedule to size the usable reservoir capacity against the 30-day refill target. As of yet, pump and tank sizing remains open to find the most suited for our use case.

5.3 Airflow and treatment-performance considerations

SPRE measures the proportion of a specified pollutant removed during one passage through the module. Single-pass clean air delivery rate (CADR) combines this efficiency with measured airflow, as defined in Appendix 2. Device-level measurements will account for leakage and bypass flow. Single-pass CADR characterises treatment through the module, while effective room treatment rate will be determined from the room experiments under defined baseline and operating conditions.

Figure 15 illustrates the potential trade-off between airflow and removal efficiency: lower airflow increases contact time, while higher airflow increases the volume processed. Experiment 1 will identify the tested operating setting that maximises CADR within the plant-airflow, moisture, noise and power constraints. The illustrated relationship is conceptual and will be evaluated experimentally.

A provisional assembly-level CADR benchmark of 350–400 m³/h was informed by particle air-cleaner sizing approaches. NEA recommends a minimum smoke CADR equivalent to five times the room volume per hour (NEA, 2024). For the largest intended room of 300 ft² (27.87 m²), an assumed ceiling height of 2.6 m gives a corresponding requirement of approximately 362 m³/h (27.87 × 2.6 × 5), within the adopted range. The AIRE calculator similarly gives approximately 350 m³/h using a rounded floor area of 27 m² and the same height and air-change rate (AIRE Purifiers, n.d.). This benchmark is a provisional assembly-level design reference, not demonstrated performance or a universal target for gaseous pollutants. Treatment capacity and the required module count will be established through pollutant-specific testing.

5.4 Baffle development and CFD findings

Three airflow configurations were compared in Ansys: no baffle and two perforated baffle designs. Without a baffle, airflow concentrated near the fan axis. Version 1 reduced this concentration but produced higher corner velocities. Version 2 showed a more uniform distribution across the 13 pot openings and was selected for prototype evaluation.

These findings remain qualitative. Quantitative assessment will compare area-averaged normal velocities across the 13 pot openings under consistent boundary conditions. Simulation assumptions are documented in Appendix 5, and prototype measurements will assess the predicted airflow distribution.

Simulated outlet velocities were approximately 0.5 m/s for a prescribed inlet velocity of 1 m/s. This comparison applies to the simulated geometry and boundary conditions; it does not establish a general 50% velocity reduction or pressure loss. Fan selection will therefore consider the assembled module’s resistance and measured operating airflow.

5.5 Fan selection and control

The ebm-papst 4890 N was initially shortlisted as a candidate fan. Final selection will consider its pressure-flow characteristics against the assembled module’s resistance and compatibility with the adjustable airflow required for Experiment 1. An alternative fan will be selected if these requirements cannot be met.

Calibration will vary the fan’s operating setting using a compatible controller; pulse-width modulation (PWM) remains an option subject to fan compatibility. Actual airflow will be measured across all pot openings rather than inferred from the control setting. Pollutant removal, noise and power consumption will inform selection of the operating point.

Subsequent treatment experiments will maintain the selected setting continuously. During normal operation, configured pollutant thresholds will switch the fan on and off at this setting.

A plant-facing air-speed limit of 1 m/s remains a provisional constraint, with suitability to be verified for the selected plant and moisture conditions.

5.6 Lighting design and calibration

Module-level lighting is proposed to improve illumination uniformity across the assembly. Preliminary measurements from the reference installation indicate photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) values of 158 μmol/m²/s near the top and 22 μmol/m²/s near the bottom, supporting the use of distributed lighting. PPFD and daily light integral (DLI), including the calculation supporting the proposed lighting range, are explained in Appendix 6.

The provisional lighting targets are a leaf-level PPFD of 40-80 μmol/m²/s, a daily photoperiod of 12–16 hours and a colour temperature of 4,000-5,000 K. The selected PPFD range is intended to support plant maintenance and moderate growth while limiting excessive foliage expansion and the associated need for pruning. These PPFD and photoperiod combinations correspond to a daily light integral of approximately 1.73-4.61 mol/m²/day. Suitability will be assessed through PPFD mapping and monitoring of plant condition.

Calibration will map PPFD across the 600 × 600 mm planting face. Lamp position and orientation will be adjusted to minimise uneven illumination and inter-module shading, with plant condition monitored during operation.

5.7 Base-module design

The base supports the assembly and houses shared services. Each assembly requires at least one hybrid base containing a water reservoir, pump and ESP32 controller. Additional water-tank bases increase storage capacity as the assembly expands horizontally.

Reservoir and pump sizing are addressed in Section 5.2 and will depend on water demand, delivery pressure and module count. The design will provide maintenance access and separate water storage from electrical components. Structural testing will assess stability and wheel loading under filled and partially filled conditions to determine whether mobility can be retained.

5.8 Sensor module and integration

The proposed sensor module combines an ESP32 controller with the following I²C-capable sensors. Calibration and system integration remain pending.

Figure 20 shows the selected sensors and their integration into the proposed control system. The sensor-module ESP32 collects readings and transmits them to the base-module ESP32, which activates the treatment fan according to configured thresholds. CO₂ provides indoor air-quality context (Sensirion, n.d.-a), while temperature and relative humidity help interpret plant conditions and sensor responses (Sensirion, n.d.-d). PM2.5 and formaldehyde measurements support air-quality monitoring (Sensirion, n.d.-b, n.d.-e), while the SGP41’s signals are processed into a VOC Index for trend monitoring (Sensirion, n.d.-c).

Quantitative removal assessment must account for the measurement limitations of the SPS30 and SFA40 sensors (Sensirion, n.d.-b, n.d.-e). Potential mist interference will also be evaluated during testing. The VOC Index does not directly represent pollutant concentration (Sensirion, n.d.-c); quantitative VOC removal testing therefore requires a separate calibrated method. Similarly, CO₂ monitoring alone does not demonstrate removal by the system.

Integration will define communication between the sensor module and base, power supply, display, data logging and fault handling. Validation will include sensor co-location, timing alignment, reference comparisons and checks for interference from mist, humidity and outlet airflow.

6. Validation plan

6.1 Validation objectives and performance metrics

The validation programme will examine three design assumptions: that a suitable fan setting provides useful pollutant removal within operating constraints; that one module maintains effective treatment across different room configurations; and that adding modules increases treatment capacity predictably. A supplementary placement comparison will assess whether near-source treatment justifies retaining mobility.

Key measurements include pollutant concentrations, airflow, single-pass removal efficiency (SPRE), single-pass clean air delivery rate (CADR), effective room treatment rate and equivalent air changes per hour (eACH). Temperature, relative humidity, plant condition, noise and power consumption will establish the associated operating constraints.

The results will inform fan operation, supported assembly configurations and room-sizing guidance. SPRE and CADR definitions are provided in Appendix 2; experimental procedures and supporting calculations are detailed in Appendix 7, Appendix 8, Appendix 9, Appendix 10.

Prototype verification will additionally assess dimensions, wet mass, footprint, irrigation capacity, lighting and control response against the requirements in Table 5.

6.2 Experiment 1: Fan flow-rate optimisation

Experiment 1, seen in Figure 21, will identify the fan setting that provides the highest supported single-pass CADR within acceptable plant-airflow, moisture, noise and power limits. It will test the proposed trade-off between airflow and removal efficiency and determine whether the selected fan and module provide a suitable operating point.

VOC source selection will build on the TVOC and PM2.5 sources seen in Table 1, which include common adhesives, cleaning products, and burning mosquito coils. These materials will first undergo pilot assessment for measurement suitability and source stability. PM2.5 testing requires a separate particle source.

Representative inlet and outlet concentrations and measured airflow will be used to determine SPRE and CADR across fan settings. Noise, power consumption and plant operating conditions will also be recorded. Quantitative VOC assessment requires a calibrated concentration measurement method; VOC Index readings alone are insufficient.

The selected setting will be retained for Experiments 2 and 3. If no setting provides adequate treatment within the operating constraints, the fan, airflow path or treatment medium will be revised. Procedures, source qualification and calculations are detailed in Appendix 7.

6.3 Experiment 2: Room-volume analysis with a continuous source

Experiment 2, seen in Figure 22, will assess how the treatment provided by one module changes as room volume increases from V to 2V and 3V. It will determine whether a common single-module treatment rate can support room-sizing guidance and how the resulting equivalent air changes per hour decrease with increasing room size.

Baseline and operating concentration histories will be measured near the source, at the module inlet and outlet, and within the occupied zone. Effective room treatment rate and equivalent Air Change per Hour (eACH) will be estimated using the continuous-source analysis in Appendix 8.

A consistent treatment rate would support a common module-capacity estimate within the tested range. If performance changes substantially, sizing guidance will account for room size and configuration. Because the source remains at the far end of each space, source-to-module distance also increases; the results therefore represent room enlargement under this placement arrangement.

A supplementary fixed-volume placement comparison will assess whether near-source positioning provides sufficient benefit to justify mobility. Procedures, model assumptions and supporting derivations are provided in Appendix 8.

6.4 Experiment 3: Module-count and treatment-rate relationship

Experiment 3, seen in Figure 23, will assess the additional treatment capacity obtained by installing more modules in a fixed room. One, two and three modules will be installed sequentially, with every installed module operating at the setting established in Experiment 1.

Concentration histories, individual module airflow and installation arrangements will be recorded. Effective room treatment rates will establish the capacity gained from each additional module and whether performance scales approximately with module count.

The results will inform whether purchasing and installing another module provides a predictable increase in room treatment capacity. Reduced gains would prompt investigation of airflow imbalance, placement or interactions between modules. Recommendations will remain limited to tested configurations, with larger installations identified as projections. The module-count procedure and scaling-efficiency calculation are detailed in Appendix 9, using the continuous-source analysis derived in Appendix 8.

Appendix 2: SPRE and CADR Definition

Single-Pass Removal Efficiency (SPRE) measures the percentage of a specified pollutant removed as air passes through the treatment system once.

SPRE (%) = [(Cᵢₙ − Cₒᵤₜ) / Cᵢₙ] × 100

Where:

Cin = pollutant concentration at the inlet.

Cout = pollutant concentration at the outlet.

Both concentrations must use the same units and be measured under corresponding operating conditions. A higher SPRE indicates greater removal per pass, but does not account for the volume of air treated.

Clean Air Delivery Rate (CADR) expresses the equivalent volume of pollutant-free air delivered by the system per unit time. For a single-pass assessment, it combines measured airflow with removal efficiency:

CADR = Q × SPRE / 100

Where:

Q = measured volumetric airflow through the treatment system (m³/h).

SPRE = single-pass removal efficiency expressed as a percentage.

SPRE and CADR are pollutant-specific and dependent on operating conditions. A higher SPRE does not necessarily produce a higher CADR if airflow is lower. Neither metric alone establishes the percentage reduction achieved throughout a room, which also depends on room volume, ventilation, pollutant sources and air mixing.

Appendix 5 Baffle Simulation Comparison

Three module airflow configurations were compared to investigate how a perforated baffle redistributes air across the planting outlets. The updated results support carrying Baffle V2 forward for prototype evaluation: its reported inner, middle and outer outlet-group velocities are more closely grouped, while the velocity contours show less central concentration than the no-baffle case. This remains a preliminary design selection pending numerical and physical verification.

Simulation Setup and Comparison Basis

The models were evaluated in Ansys Fluent 26.1 using a three-dimensional, steady, pressure-based solver with SST k–ω turbulence modelling. Air density was 1.225 kg/m³ and dynamic viscosity was 1.7894 × 10⁻⁵ kg/(m·s). Coupled pressure–velocity solution and second-order spatial schemes were used. Appendix 6 Lighting metrics and supporting calculations

Appendix 6.1 Photosynthetic photon flux density

Photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) measures the rate at which photons within the conventional photosynthetically active radiation range of 400–700 nm reach a surface. It is expressed in micromoles of photons per square metre per second (μmol/m²/s). PPFD therefore describes the light received at a measurement position, rather than the lamp’s electrical power or colour temperature (Apogee Instruments, n.d.).

PPFD will be measured at representative leaf positions across the planting face using a suitable quantum sensor. Measurement position, sensor orientation and lamp distance will be recorded. Minimum, maximum and mean values will describe illumination distribution.

Appendix 6.2 Daily light integral

Daily light integral (DLI) is the total photosynthetically active photon exposure received per unit area over a day, expressed in mol/m²/day (Apogee Instruments, n.d.).

For constant PPFD during the illuminated period:

DLI = PPFD × photoperiod × 3600 / 1,000,000

Here, photoperiod is expressed in hours per day. The factors 3600 and 1,000,000 convert hours to seconds and micromoles to moles, respectively.

For the lower proposed combination:

DLI = 40 × 12 × 3600 / 1,000,000
DLI = 1.728 ≈ 1.73 mol/m²/day

For the upper proposed combination:

DLI = 80 × 16 × 3600 / 1,000,000
DLI = 4.608 ≈ 4.61 mol/m²/day

These values describe the proposed design range, not a verified biological optimum. Where daylight or lamp output varies, DLI must account for the changing PPFD throughout the day.

Appendix 7 Experiment 1 — Fan flow-rate optimisation

Appendix 7.1 Experimental procedure

Candidate challenge pollutants include formaldehyde, other selected VOCs and PM2.5. The final pollutants, emission methods and test facilities will be confirmed with the laboratory supervisor, considering the potential sources listed in Table 1. Each test will use a concentration measurement method suitable for the selected pollutant and test conditions. Formaldehyde removal will be assessed separately from other VOCs. VOC Index readings will support trend monitoring but will not be used to calculate SPRE or CADR.

Assemble one planted module with the intended fan, baffle, seals and growing medium.

Verify sensor agreement through co-location and reference comparison where available. Document instrument uncertainty and response time.

Establish a stable pollutant stream through the module, with representative upstream and downstream sampling.

Test selected PWM settings across the proposed 25–100% range.

After airflow and concentration signals stabilise, record inlet and outlet concentrations, actual airflow, temperature, relative humidity, electrical power and noise.

Conduct at least three independent runs per setting, subject to pilot-run variability and available facilities.

Substrate moisture, irrigation timing and plant condition will be documented. Sampling will represent the overall treatment flow rather than individual high-performing outlets. Leakage and bypass paths will be included in the device-level assessment.

Appendix 7.2 Airflow calculation

Airflow will be calculated from area-averaged velocities normal to the measured openings:

Q = 3600 × Σ(uᵢ × Aᵢ) (Eqn Appendix 7.1)

Where:

Q = total measured airflow, m³/h.

uᵢ = area-averaged normal velocity at opening i, m/s.

Aᵢ = area of opening i, m².

Σ = summation across all measured openings.

3600 = conversion from seconds to hours.

Each opening will be sampled sufficiently to represent its velocity distribution. Leakage flows will be assessed separately where they are not captured by the measured openings.

Appendix 7.3 SPRE and single-pass CADR

The relationships defined in Appendix 2 are applied as follows:

η = (Cᵢₙ − Cₒᵤₜ) / Cᵢₙ (Eqn Appendix 7.2)
SPRE (%) = 100 × η (Eqn Appendix 7.3)
CADR_SP = Q × η = Q × SPRE / 100 (Eqn Appendix 7.4)

Where:

Cᵢₙ = representative inlet pollutant concentration.

Cₒᵤₜ = representative outlet pollutant concentration.

η = removal efficiency expressed as a fraction.

CADR_SP = single-pass clean air delivery rate, m³/h.

Concentrations must refer to the same pollutant, use consistent units and represent corresponding operating conditions. Where outlet concentrations vary substantially, mixed-outlet sampling or flow-weighted concentrations will be required.

Appendix 7.4 Operating-point selection

SPRE and CADR_SP will be plotted against measured airflow. The selected setting will provide the highest supported CADR_SP within the provisional plant-airflow constraint and acceptable moisture, noise and power conditions.

Results will include repeat-run variability and measurement uncertainty. A unique optimum will not be claimed where differences between settings cannot be resolved. The selected setting will be retained for subsequent experiments unless design changes require re-characterisation.

Appendix 8: Experiment 2 — Room-configuration analysis with a continuous source

Appendix 8.1 Experimental procedure

Three adjacent spaces of equal volume will be combined progressively by removing partitions to create V, 2V and 3V. Actual dimensions and accessible air volumes will be recorded.

One module will remain at a fixed position. The pollutant source will be placed at the far end of each configuration, following the intended room-scaling arrangement. HVAC settings, furniture, doors and source preparation will be controlled and documented.

Each configuration will receive matched baseline and operating runs. During operating runs, the fan will remain continuously at the setting selected in Experiment 1, with automatic threshold switching disabled.

Concentrations will be recorded near the source, at the module inlet and outlet, and at a documented occupied-zone position. Recording will capture a resolvable transient towards equilibrium. Sampling duration and frequency will be established through pilot tests.

At least three independent runs per condition are proposed. Source stability and measurement suitability must be demonstrated over the fitted interval. Continued evaporation from a material will not, by itself, be treated as evidence of constant emissions.

Room enlargement changes both source distance and potentially air circulation. These effects will be considered when interpreting changes in effective treatment rate.

Appendix 8.2 Baseline definition

The primary baseline will retain the planted module in position with its treatment fan switched off. Plant condition, irrigation and lighting will remain matched to the operating run. The estimated treatment rate will therefore represent the additional effect of active operation relative to the inactive installation.

This comparison does not independently quantify passive removal or isolate the contribution of plants from the growing medium and other components. A separate absent-module comparison would be required to assess the complete installation against an empty-room baseline.

Appendix 8.3 Continuous-source mass balance

For a sufficiently well-mixed room:

Accumulation = Pollutant input − Pollutant removal
V × dC/dt = G + Qᵥ × Cₛᵤₚₚₗᵧ − (Qᵥ + kₛ × V + D) × C (Eqn Appendix 8.1)

Where:

C = representative room pollutant concentration, µg/m³.

t = time, h.

V = room volume, m³.

G = indoor pollutant emission rate, µg/h.

Qᵥ = effective ventilation flow, m³/h.

Cₛᵤₚₚₗᵧ = supply-air pollutant concentration, µg/m³.

kₛ = first-order background loss coefficient, h⁻¹.

D = additional effective treatment rate relative to the selected baseline, m³/h.

Other concentration units may be used if all terms are converted consistently. The ventilation term assumes balanced supply and exhaust flows. Background losses may include deposition and passive uptake where these can reasonably be represented as first-order processes.

The model assumes approximately constant emissions, ventilation, supply concentration and removal coefficients over the fitted interval.

Appendix 8.4 Equilibrium concentration

At equilibrium:

dC/dt = 0

Substituting into Equation Appendix 8.1:

G + Qᵥ × Cₛᵤₚₚₗᵧ = (Qᵥ + kₛ × V + D) × Cₛₛ

Therefore:

Cₛₛ = (G + Qᵥ × Cₛᵤₚₚₗᵧ) / (Qᵥ + kₛ × V + D) (Eqn Appendix 8.2)

Cₛₛ is the equilibrium concentration. With continuing pollutant input, this concentration is generally non-zero.

Appendix 8.5 Time-dependent concentration

Define the overall rate coefficient:

K = (Qᵥ + kₛ × V + D) / V (Eqn Appendix 8.3)

Dividing Equation Appendix 8.1 by V and substituting Equation Appendix 8.2 gives:

dC/dt = −K × (C − Cₛₛ) (Eqn Appendix 8.4)

Separating variables and integrating:

ln|(C(t) − Cₛₛ) / (C(0) − Cₛₛ)| = −K × t

Hence:

C(t) = Cₛₛ + [C(0) − Cₛₛ] × exp(−K × t) (Eqn Appendix 8.5)

Where:

C(0) = concentration at the start of the analysed interval.

C(t) = concentration at time t.

K = rate coefficient, h⁻¹.

exp(−K × t) = e raised to the power −K × t.

Equation Appendix 8.5 describes an increase or decrease towards equilibrium, depending on the initial concentration. If C(0) equals Cₛₛ, concentration remains constant. A source-off equation assuming decay towards zero is unsuitable while the pollutant source continues emitting.

Appendix 8.6 Effective treatment rate

For the baseline, additional active treatment is defined as D = 0:

K_off = Qᵥ / V + kₛ (Eqn Appendix 8.6)

With the module operating:

K_on = Qᵥ / V + kₛ + D / V (Eqn Appendix 8.7)

If ventilation and background losses remain unchanged:

K_on − K_off = D / V

Therefore:

D = V × (K_on − K_off) (Eqn Appendix 8.8)

Equivalent air changes per hour are:

eACH = D / V (Eqn Appendix 8.9)

D is expressed in m³/h and eACH in h⁻¹.

Appendix 8.7 Fitting and interpretation

Equation Appendix 8.5 will be fitted to suitable concentration histories to estimate K and Cₛₛ. Measurements must contain a resolvable transient; equilibrium measurements alone cannot independently identify K.

Model suitability will be assessed using spatial agreement, fitted residuals, source stability and repeatability. Strong concentration gradients, changing adsorption behaviour or insufficient sensor response may prevent a reliable room-wide estimate.

If the model is unsupported, location-specific concentration histories and reductions will be reported. If K_on − K_off cannot be resolved within uncertainty, a reliable positive treatment rate will not be claimed.

Appendix 8.8 Supplementary placement and mobility assessment

Within one fixed room configuration, the module will be tested near the source and at a more distant position. Source output, HVAC operation, furniture and fan setting will remain consistent. Intake orientation will be documented.

Room-monitoring locations will remain fixed, while device-level inlet and outlet sampling will follow the module. Concentrations will be compared over a common observation period.

Time-integrated concentration is:

I꜀ = ∫₀ᵀ C(t) dt (Eqn Appendix 8.10)

For discrete measurements, the trapezoidal approximation is:

I꜀ ≈ Σ{[(Cᵢ + Cᵢ₊₁) / 2] × (tᵢ₊₁ − tᵢ)} (Eqn Appendix 8.11)

Where T is the common observation duration. With concentration in µg/m³ and time in hours, I꜀ has units of µg·h/m³.

Lower integrated concentration indicates lower cumulative concentration at that monitoring location during the test; it does not independently establish room-wide CADR.

A movement trial will assess turning clearance, required operators, service disconnection and reconnection, leakage and stability. Treatment benefit and movement feasibility will jointly inform whether wheels are retained.

Appendix 9: Experiment 3 — Module-count and treatment-rate relationship

Appendix 9.1 Experimental procedure

The experiment will use a fixed room volume and progressively install one, two and three planted modules. All installed modules will operate continuously at the setting selected in Experiment 1. Module locations, spacing and orientation will be documented.

Source preparation, HVAC operation, room-monitoring positions and plant conditioning will remain consistent. Individual module airflow will be verified after each installation.

Each installed configuration will receive a matched fan-off baseline with the same planted modules present. This separates the additional effect of active operation from changes in passive surfaces introduced by adding modules. The baseline is a measurement control; module count is changed by physically adding modules.

Concentration histories will be collected for baseline and operating conditions, with at least three independent runs per condition proposed. Effective treatment rates will be calculated using Appendix 8 where its assumptions are supported.

An additional no-module reference may be used to compare occupied-zone concentrations before and after installation. Such comparisons will be reported separately from the active-treatment rates derived using configuration-specific baselines.

Appendix 9.2 Treatment-rate calculation

For each active module count:

Dₙ = V × (K_on,n − K_off,n) (Eqn Appendix 9.1)

Where:

Dₙ = effective treatment rate with n active modules, m³/h.

V = fixed room volume, m³.

K_on,n = fitted operating rate coefficient, h⁻¹.

K_off,n = corresponding baseline rate coefficient, h⁻¹.

A common baseline may be used only where the physical arrangement and background conditions remain sufficiently consistent.

Incremental capacity from additional modules

The additional active treatment capacity associated with installing the second and third modules is:

ΔD₂ = D₂ − D₁
ΔD₃ = D₃ − D₂

For fixed room volume V, the corresponding increases in equivalent air changes per hour are:

ΔeACH₂ = (D₂ − D₁) / V
ΔeACH₃ = (D₃ − D₂) / V

These quantities describe the measured gain from each installation step under the tested arrangement. Scaling efficiency remains:

εₙ = Dₙ / (n × D₁)

Both incremental capacity and scaling efficiency will be interpreted with measurement uncertainty. Because Dₙ uses a matched inactive-installation baseline, it represents additional active treatment rather than the total passive and active effect of the installed system.

Appendix 9.3 Scaling efficiency

For a measured single-module treatment rate D₁, ideal additive performance predicts:

Dₙ,ideal = n × D₁ (Eqn Appendix 9.2)

Scaling efficiency is:

εₙ = Dₙ / (n × D₁) (Eqn Appendix 9.3)

For two modules:

ε₂ = D₂ / (2 × D₁)

For three modules:

ε₃ = D₃ / (3 × D₁)

Percentage scaling efficiency is:

Scaling efficiency (%) = 100 × εₙ (Eqn Appendix 9.4)

Values near one, or 100%, indicate approximately additive performance. Departures will be examined alongside measurement uncertainty, source variability, individual airflow and module interactions.

Scaling efficiency will only be interpreted when D₁ is positive and sufficiently resolved. Testing up to three modules will not establish the performance of larger assemblies.

Appendix 10: Sizing-guide calculations and interpretation

Appendix 10.1 Room-volume calculation

For floor area A and ceiling height H:

V = A × H (Eqn Appendix 10.1)

Where:

V = room volume, m³.

A = floor area, m².

H = ceiling height, m.

Floor area expressed in square feet will be converted using:

A(m²) = 0.092903 × A(ft²) (Eqn Appendix 10.2)

Appendix 10.2 Equivalent air changes per hour

For measured effective treatment rate Dₙ:

eACHₙ = Dₙ / V (Eqn Appendix 10.3)
Substituting V = A × H:
eACHₙ = Dₙ / (A × H) (Eqn Appendix 10.4)

Where Dₙ is in m³/h and eACHₙ is in h⁻¹. These values represent equivalent treatment capacity, not outdoor-air ventilation.

If treatment rate remains constant:

eACH at volume V = Dₙ / V
eACH at volume 2V = Dₙ / (2 × V)
eACH at volume 3V = Dₙ / (3 × V)

Doubling or tripling room volume therefore reduces eACH to one-half or one-third respectively. Experiment 2 will assess whether the constant-treatment-rate assumption is supported.

Appendix 10.3 Required treatment capacity

For an adopted target eACH:

D_required = eACH_target × V (Eqn Appendix 10.5)

Or:

D_required = eACH_target × A × H (Eqn Appendix 10.6)

The target must have a stated pollutant and application basis. Configuration recommendations will compare D_required with measured treatment rates and their uncertainty.

Appendix 10.4 Module-count projections

Where additive scaling is supported, a preliminary module-count estimate is:

n_estimated = ceiling(D_required / D₁) (Eqn Appendix 10.7)

Here, ceiling means rounding upwards to the nearest whole module.

This calculation assumes each additional module provides the same effective treatment rate as the first. Measured multi-module results will take precedence. Estimates beyond the tested range will be labelled as projections and checked against structural and service limits.

If Experiment 2 identifies substantial room-configuration effects, sizing recommendations will use configuration-specific treatment rates rather than one universal D₁.

Appendix 10.5 Limits of the sizing guide

The guide will state the tested pollutant, operating setting, ceiling height, room conditions, uncertainty and supported module arrangements.

Equal eACH does not guarantee equal equilibrium concentrations. As derived in Appendix 8:

Cₛₛ = (G + Qᵥ × Cₛᵤₚₚₗᵧ) / (Qᵥ + kₛ × V + D)

Final concentrations also depend on pollutant emissions, incoming-air quality, ventilation and background removal. The guide will therefore describe measured treatment capacity without implying a guaranteed concentration or health outcome.
