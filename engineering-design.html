5.1 Plant-module construction

Each module has a 600 mm × 600 mm planting face and a 250 mm-deep enclosure, as shown in Figure 13. Thirteen 80 mm-diameter pots are arranged in alternating rows of three and two. A rear fan supplies air through an internal chamber and perforated baffle before it passes through the planted pots. Module-level lighting is supported in front of the planting face; its projection is additional to the enclosure depth.

Construction combines laser-cut and 3D-printed components. Prototype assembly will verify pot fit, sealing, maintenance access and load transfer through the supporting structure under wet operating conditions. Development of the inter-module connection, including linkage and rack-and-pinion motion transfer and alternative interlocking interfaces, is documented in Appendix 4. The estimated module mass (derived from SolidWorks) is 17.5 kg excluding plants. Component-level mass accounting and wet-module weighing will establish whether the complete module meets the provisional 25 kg handling target.

5.2 Irrigation design

The irrigation system supplies water from the base reservoir through a shared pump and inter-module pipe connectors and pipework to misting nozzles within each plant module as seen in Figure 14. The staggered pot arrangement provides access for mist distribution, with watering uniformity to be verified experimentally.

Pump selection will account for delivery pressure, nozzle demand and the number and elevation of connected modules. Usable reservoir capacity will be assessed against measured consumption and losses to evaluate the 30-day refill target. Drainage, flow management, leakage detection and reservoir monitoring remain under development.

Calibration will establish nozzle flow, misting duration and cycle intervals. Airflow tests will use defined substrate moisture conditions, with irrigation timing, temperature and relative humidity recorded. Outlet velocities will be checked across the expected operating conditions against the provisional plant-airflow limit.

Net water consumption will be measured under the intended irrigation schedule to size the usable reservoir capacity against the 30-day refill target. As of yet, pump and tank sizing remains open to find the most suited for our use case.

5.3 Airflow and treatment-performance considerations

SPRE measures the proportion of a specified pollutant removed during one passage through the module. Single-pass clean air delivery rate (CADR) combines this efficiency with measured airflow, as defined in Appendix 2. Device-level measurements will account for leakage and bypass flow. Single-pass CADR characterises treatment through the module, while effective room treatment rate will be determined from the room experiments under defined baseline and operating conditions.

Figure 15 illustrates the potential trade-off between airflow and removal efficiency: lower airflow increases contact time, while higher airflow increases the volume processed. Experiment 1 will identify the tested operating setting that maximises CADR within the plant-airflow, moisture, noise and power constraints. The illustrated relationship is conceptual and will be evaluated experimentally.

A provisional assembly-level CADR benchmark of 350–400 m³/h was informed by particle air-cleaner sizing approaches. NEA recommends a minimum smoke CADR equivalent to five times the room volume per hour (NEA, 2024). For the largest intended room of 300 ft² (27.87 m²), an assumed ceiling height of 2.6 m gives a corresponding requirement of approximately 362 m³/h (27.87 × 2.6 × 5), within the adopted range. The AIRE calculator similarly gives approximately 350 m³/h using a rounded floor area of 27 m² and the same height and air-change rate (AIRE Purifiers, n.d.). This benchmark is a provisional assembly-level design reference, not demonstrated performance or a universal target for gaseous pollutants. Treatment capacity and the required module count will be established through pollutant-specific testing.

5.4 Baffle development and CFD findings

Three airflow configurations were compared in Ansys: no baffle and two perforated baffle designs. Without a baffle, airflow concentrated near the fan axis. Version 1 reduced this concentration but produced higher corner velocities. Version 2 showed a more uniform distribution across the 13 pot openings and was selected for prototype evaluation.

These findings remain qualitative. Quantitative assessment will compare area-averaged normal velocities across the 13 pot openings under consistent boundary conditions. Simulation assumptions are documented in Appendix 5, and prototype measurements will assess the predicted airflow distribution.

Simulated outlet velocities were approximately 0.5 m/s for a prescribed inlet velocity of 1 m/s. This comparison applies to the simulated geometry and boundary conditions; it does not establish a general 50% velocity reduction or pressure loss. Fan selection will therefore consider the assembled module’s resistance and measured operating airflow.

5.5 Fan selection and control

The ebm-papst 4890 N was initially shortlisted as a candidate fan. Final selection will consider its pressure-flow characteristics against the assembled module’s resistance and compatibility with the adjustable airflow required for Experiment 1. An alternative fan will be selected if these requirements cannot be met.

Calibration will vary the fan’s operating setting using a compatible controller; pulse-width modulation (PWM) remains an option subject to fan compatibility. Actual airflow will be measured across all pot openings rather than inferred from the control setting. Pollutant removal, noise and power consumption will inform selection of the operating point.

Subsequent treatment experiments will maintain the selected setting continuously. During normal operation, configured pollutant thresholds will switch the fan on and off at this setting.

A plant-facing air-speed limit of 1 m/s remains a provisional constraint, with suitability to be verified for the selected plant and moisture conditions.

5.6 Lighting design and calibration

Module-level lighting is proposed to improve illumination uniformity across the assembly. Preliminary measurements from the reference installation indicate photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) values of 158 μmol/m²/s near the top and 22 μmol/m²/s near the bottom, supporting the use of distributed lighting. PPFD and daily light integral (DLI), including the calculation supporting the proposed lighting range, are explained in Appendix 6.

The provisional lighting targets are a leaf-level PPFD of 40-80 μmol/m²/s, a daily photoperiod of 12–16 hours and a colour temperature of 4,000-5,000 K. The selected PPFD range is intended to support plant maintenance and moderate growth while limiting excessive foliage expansion and the associated need for pruning. These PPFD and photoperiod combinations correspond to a daily light integral of approximately 1.73-4.61 mol/m²/day. Suitability will be assessed through PPFD mapping and monitoring of plant condition.

Calibration will map PPFD across the 600 × 600 mm planting face. Lamp position and orientation will be adjusted to minimise uneven illumination and inter-module shading, with plant condition monitored during operation.

5.7 Base-module design

The base supports the assembly and houses shared services. Each assembly requires at least one hybrid base containing a water reservoir, pump and ESP32 controller. Additional water-tank bases increase storage capacity as the assembly expands horizontally.

Reservoir and pump sizing are addressed in Section 5.2 and will depend on water demand, delivery pressure and module count. The design will provide maintenance access and separate water storage from electrical components. Structural testing will assess stability and wheel loading under filled and partially filled conditions to determine whether mobility can be retained.

5.8 Sensor module and integration

The proposed sensor module combines an ESP32 controller with the following I²C-capable sensors. Calibration and system integration remain pending.

Figure 20 shows the selected sensors and their integration into the proposed control system. The sensor-module ESP32 collects readings and transmits them to the base-module ESP32, which activates the treatment fan according to configured thresholds. CO₂ provides indoor air-quality context (Sensirion, n.d.-a), while temperature and relative humidity help interpret plant conditions and sensor responses (Sensirion, n.d.-d). PM2.5 and formaldehyde measurements support air-quality monitoring (Sensirion, n.d.-b, n.d.-e), while the SGP41’s signals are processed into a VOC Index for trend monitoring (Sensirion, n.d.-c).

Quantitative removal assessment must account for the measurement limitations of the SPS30 and SFA40 sensors (Sensirion, n.d.-b, n.d.-e). Potential mist interference will also be evaluated during testing. The VOC Index does not directly represent pollutant concentration (Sensirion, n.d.-c); quantitative VOC removal testing therefore requires a separate calibrated method. Similarly, CO₂ monitoring alone does not demonstrate removal by the system.

Integration will define communication between the sensor module and base, power supply, display, data logging and fault handling. Validation will include sensor co-location, timing alignment, reference comparisons and checks for interference from mist, humidity and outlet airflow.
